# RAGs to Riches

*Volume IV — From Text to Intelligence · Concept 011*

**A chunk is not an island.**

The worked example follows one corpus through segmentation, enrichment, retrieval and generation. All six passages are fictional. The candidate set, projection and response are illustrations.

## 00–09 seconds · A chunk is not an island

The Volume IV title and subtitle give way to fictional Document A. Two dashed retrieval units and two abstract embedding glyphs appear beneath the intact military account.

**Caption**  What if the knowledge is already there?

**0.45 s**  Retrieval-augmented generation often begins with chunks of text. What if readers have already connected those words to structured knowledge?

## 09–23 seconds · Across the cut

Napoleon and He occupy different initial chunks. Both marks lead to a single Napoleon Bonaparte. A proposed cut moves inside the annotated pointing action. The next position keeps the action together. Other annotations may still cross a boundary.

**Caption**  Separate chunks. Preserved connections.

**9.25 s**  Across a chunk boundary, the name Napoleon and the pronoun he still refer to the same person.

**16.60 s**  A reader’s annotation can even suggest where to place the boundary.

## 23–42 seconds · What reaches the embedding model

Document B and a derived text-only representation enter the frame. Follow the existing annotations on The Emperor, Paris and the return journey into the separate enriched record. The source stays above it. The embedding glyph changes as the model input changes.

**Caption**  One passage to read. A derived representation to embed.

**23.45 s**  Take this sentence about the Emperor. We can embed its words as they stand.

**29.90 s**  The annotations already identify the person, the place and the return journey. A retrieval representation can include that knowledge, while the original sentence stays on the page.

## 42–51 seconds · Three readings of one passage

The historical, rhetorical and literary annotations supply three possible representations. Their source words, ranges and classifications are visible. Only one instance of Document B sits above the three readings.

**Caption**  One text. Multiple semantic perspectives.

**42.25 s**  The same words can support different readings. Historical, rhetorical and literary annotations can each inform a retrieval representation.

## 51–66 seconds · A question and its candidates

The question asks how observers characterised Napoleon’s military leadership. It enters an embedding model. An illustrative projection highlights B, C and F. Selecting Napoleon keeps B and C. The Emperor in F resolves to Augustus.

**Caption**  Semantic similarity meets explicit identity.

**51.40 s**  How did observers characterise Napoleon’s military leadership? Similarity can suggest passages.

**59.00 s**  Select Napoleon. The title Emperor names him in one passage, and Augustus in another.

## 66–93 seconds · The connected chunk

Begin with C. Its text reveals the standoff ranges for Our commander and decisive. Follow Mention C1 to Napoleon and the judgement attributed to Officer A. Move along the next selected connection to D and Aide B’s different judgement. E adds an order, troops and a qualification. The optional observer or annotated-range filter foreshadows a narrower selection.

**Caption**  Similarity discovers. Structure connects.

**66.35 s**  One candidate calls the commander decisive. Follow that mention, and the chunk opens onto a graph.

**73.35 s**  The same person is linked to a second account. The aide calls him reckless. Each judgement has an observer and its own passage.

**83.00 s**  Another path leads to a military order, its participants and its source.

**89.10 s**  Similarity discovers. Structure connects.

## 93–109 seconds · Assemble the evidence

The three passages keep their identities as the records move into a shared context. Read the two observer credits beside the trait expressions, then follow the military order to its recipient, action and timing. Napoleon is the subject of both traits and the issuer of the order.

**Caption**  Textual evidence + structured context.

**93.35 s**  Gather the selected passages with their source references. Keep the officer’s and the aide’s judgements attached to the people who made them.

**102.65 s**  The order supplies a different kind of evidence. Its roles and timing can travel with the words.

## 109–120 seconds · Supply the question and context

The question and a serialised context enter the model. A short illustrative response attributes each description and points to C and D. The qualification about experiment stays visible.

**Caption**  How effective is it? An empirical question.

**109.25 s**  The question and this context can now be supplied to a language model. How effective this approach is remains a question for experiment.

## 120–132 seconds · A doorway into knowledge

C briefly returns as a chunk linked to Napoleon. The series engraving and volume bookend hold the closing line.

**120.45 s**  A chunk need not be an isolated fragment of text.

**125.50 s**  It can be a doorway into a network of knowledge.
