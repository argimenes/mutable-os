# Mutable — Concept Explainer
# Mutability

## 1. Production brief

Produce a flagship animated concept explainer exploring the principle of mutability as the philosophical and architectural foundation of Mutable.

This video should draw together several ideas established in previous explainers while introducing a substantial new territory: the freedom of intellectual objects to acquire new forms, contexts, structures and capabilities through composition.

This is not a product demonstration, a feature catalogue, an architectural tutorial or a retrospective compilation of previous videos.

It is an exploration of a design philosophy, communicated through concrete, visually intelligible transformations.

### Duration

Do not impose an arbitrary duration limit.

Allow the material to determine the length of the video. Each important transformation should have sufficient time to establish its initial state, unfold intelligibly and reveal its intellectual significance.

Prefer clarity, contemplation and conceptual completeness over brevity.

A duration of approximately six to nine minutes may be appropriate, but this is an indicative range rather than a production constraint. Extend the video if the material requires it.

### Production workflow

Use the established code-driven animation framework, production conventions and reusable animation assets of the preceding Mutable concept explainers.

Use the installed `teaching-by-example` skill to guide narration and pedagogical presentation.

Introduce the new visual register described below while maintaining continuity with the intellectual and artistic identity of the existing series.

---

## 2. Central thesis

**Mutability is not merely the freedom to change things. It is the freedom to change what things can become.**

Conventional software frequently allows us to modify objects within predefined structures and categories.

Mutable explores a complementary possibility: intellectual objects can acquire new contexts, relationships, surfaces, structures and capabilities through composition, without necessarily surrendering their original nature.

An image can become more than an image while remaining an image.

A video can acquire the scholarly apparatus of a document while remaining a video.

A conversation can become an object of sustained intellectual investigation while retaining its identity as a conversation.

A document can become a constituent of another document.

The common block architecture provides the foundation for these possibilities.

### Supporting philosophical principles

- An object's identity need not be exhausted by its present representation.
- An object's capabilities need not be exhausted by its original type.
- An object's context need not determine its permanent location.
- A composition need not be the final destiny of its constituents.
- Enrichment need not entail replacement.
- Complexity need not entail fragmentation.
- Intellectual structures should remain open to further transformation.

The video should make these principles visible through animation rather than present them as a succession of abstract propositions.

---

## 3. Conceptual framework

The explainer should explore several interrelated forms of mutability.

| Form | Principle |
|---|---|
| Textual mutability | Text can change while its annotations persist and adapt. |
| Semantic mutability | New meanings and interpretations can be associated with existing material. |
| Spatial mutability | Arrangement can become a medium of intellectual expression. |
| Compositional mutability | Objects can be assembled, separated, wrapped and recombined. |
| Functional mutability | Objects can acquire new capabilities through composition. |
| Contextual mutability | Objects can participate in different intellectual environments. |
| Presentational mutability | The same material can support different ways of being experienced. |

These categories are a conceptual framework for production, not necessarily a seven-part taxonomy to be presented explicitly to the viewer.

Individual scenes may demonstrate several forms of mutability simultaneously.

The viewer should gradually discover their common foundation.

---

## 4. Visual direction: a new register

### From abstraction to composition

The earlier explainers have predominantly used an abstract visual language: text, annotations, entities, ranges and relationships presented against a dark background.

That register remains appropriate when briefly revisiting textual and semantic mutability.

However, the principal subject of this explainer is the intellectual object as something possessing boundaries, surfaces, internal structure and spatial relationships.

Introduce a complementary, page-based visual register.

### Aesthetic

Aim for the appearance of a beautifully typeset scholarly publication or editorial composition situated within a neutral, abstract studio environment.

Consider:

- Warm ivory or subtly off-white document surfaces.
- A restrained, neutral surrounding canvas.
- Charcoal typography.
- Carefully controlled accent colours.
- Subtle shadows and spatial depth.
- Elegant serif typography for document prose, where appropriate.
- Continuity with the established series typography for explanatory labels and titles.

Do not reproduce the colour scheme or interface chrome of Flint, Dialectic, Dossier or any other Mutable application.

This is a pedagogical visual language, not a proposed application theme.

Avoid excessive skeuomorphism, simulated leather bindings, ornamental paper textures or realistic desktop clutter.

The document should possess enough physicality to make its spatial transformations intelligible, but enough abstraction to perform operations impossible on paper.

### The governing visual principle

**The viewer must never lose sight of the original object.**

When an image acquires additional surfaces, its identity as an image should remain recognisable.

When a document becomes a page within a larger composition, its contents should visibly persist.

When a video acquires a transcript and references, the original video should remain recognisable throughout.

Use persistent visual identifiers, consistent colours, recognisable content and continuous motion to communicate continuity through transformation.

Avoid unexplained substitutions in which one object simply disappears and another appears.

# 5. Storyboard

## ACT I — WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO BE MUTABLE?

### Scene 01 — The question

**Visuals**

Open in the familiar abstract visual register of the preceding explainers.

Present the title:

**Mutability**

Introduce a short textual passage.

A word changes. Another expression is inserted. A sentence grows.

Initially, this resembles ordinary text editing.

Then reveal the standoff annotations associated with the passage.

As the underlying text changes, the annotations adapt while retaining their semantic associations.

Briefly recall the established visual vocabulary of *When Text Changes*.

The animation slows.

Present the question:

**Is mutability simply the ability to change something?**

**Narration**

> What does it mean for something to be mutable?
>
> At first, the answer seems obvious.
>
> Something is mutable if it can be changed.
>
> A sentence can be revised. A paragraph can grow. A document can be rewritten.
>
> And, as we have seen, even the semantic structures associated with a text can persist as that text changes.
>
> But perhaps mutability can mean something more.

**Pedagogical objective**

Begin with the familiar meaning of mutability and establish continuity with the preceding series.

Prepare the viewer for a broader conception without immediately introducing architectural terminology.

---

### Scene 02 — Meaning without replacement

**Visuals**

Return to the passage.

Introduce several standoff annotations representing different interpretations or ontologies.

Allow these semantic structures to accumulate without rewriting the underlying text.

Briefly reveal entity connections.

Recall the distinction between textual expression and semantic identity established in *What's in a Name?*

Then show a simplified entity-focused document transformation recalling *A Document in Concert*.

The original document contracts around an entity and subsequently expands again.

These recollections should be concise and visually economical.

The text persists through semantic enrichment and changes of presentation.

**Narration**

> A text can acquire new meanings without being rewritten.
>
> A document can acquire new ways of being read without losing its original composition.
>
> What changes is not always the object itself.
>
> Sometimes, what changes is what we can do with it, how we understand it, or how it is presented to us.

**Pedagogical objective**

Recall textual, semantic and presentational mutability.

Do not re-explain the earlier videos in detail.

Establish the recurring principle of persistence through change.

---

### Scene 03 — The limits of categories

**Visuals**

Transition from the dark abstract register into the new page-based register.

The surrounding visual field gradually becomes a neutral, light studio environment.

Introduce several recognisable digital objects:

- A paragraph.
- An image.
- A video.
- A document.
- A conversation.

Initially, each appears as an independent object.

Briefly associate each with its conventional function:

Text — read and edit.

Image — view.

Video — play.

Document — compose.

Conversation — participate and revisit.

These labels should appear as familiar expectations, not immutable definitions.

Then allow the labels to recede.

The objects remain.

**Narration**

> We are accustomed to thinking of digital objects in terms of their categories.
>
> An image is something we view. A video is something we play. A document is something we read and edit.
>
> These distinctions are useful.
>
> But must they also determine the limits of what those objects can become?
>
> What if an image could acquire some of the capabilities of a document?
>
> What if a conversation could become an intellectual workspace?
>
> What if the boundaries between these categories were more permeable?

**Pedagogical objective**

Introduce the limitations of treating an object's original category as the boundary of its expressive possibilities.

Avoid claiming that other software cannot combine media or enrich objects.

The argument concerns the generality and composability of the operations.

## ACT II — THE BLOCK AS PRIMITIVE

### Scene 04 — The common foundation

**Visuals**

Return to the independent objects.

Introduce a subtle, consistent boundary around each.

The paragraph, image, video and conversation are revealed as different kinds of blocks or block compositions.

Their contents remain visibly distinct.

Do not make them look as though they have been converted into a common media format.

Instead, reveal a shared compositional vocabulary.

Several blocks assemble into a document.

A second arrangement assembles into a different document.

An individual block is extracted from the first arrangement and becomes independent.

It is then embedded within the second arrangement.

The surrounding document accommodates it.

The block's recognisable content persists throughout.

**Narration**

> In Mutable, the block is a fundamental unit of composition.
>
> A block may contain text, an image, a video or a more elaborate intellectual structure.
>
> A document is an arrangement of such blocks.
>
> But a block need not be permanently defined by the document in which it happens to reside.
>
> It can become an independent object, or participate in another composition.
>
> Its context can change without its nature necessarily changing.

**Pedagogical objective**

Communicate compositional independence.

The document is not merely a receptacle that permanently owns its contents.

The block is a primitive that can participate in different arrangements.

Avoid technical discussion of classes, schemas, serialization or implementation details.

---

### Scene 05 — Composition and decomposition

**Visuals**

Introduce a document containing a mixture of text, images and other recognisable blocks.

The document gradually separates into its constituent objects.

Each retains its appearance and recognisable content.

The blocks become individually available for further operations.

Some return to their original arrangement.

Others form a different arrangement.

A small composition becomes part of a larger one.

The larger composition subsequently separates again.

The viewer should perceive composition and decomposition as complementary operations.

Introduce the statement:

**An arrangement need not be the permanent destiny of its contents.**

**Narration**

> We can assemble individual objects into a larger intellectual whole.
>
> But we can also recover the constituent parts of that whole.
>
> Composition need not be a terminal operation.
>
> What has been assembled can become available for further arrangement.
>
> The same material can participate in new structures as our intellectual purposes change.

**Pedagogical objective**

Establish the open-ended nature of the compositional model.

This is not merely about cutting and pasting content.

It is about preserving the availability of intellectual objects for further transformation.

## ACT III — BECOMING MORE THAN ONE WAS

### Scene 06 — More than an image

**Visuals**

Introduce an independent JPEG depicting an archival photograph, engraving or other visually distinctive subject.

The image appears as a simple, one-sided object.

Beside it, introduce some associated research material:

- Source.
- Date.
- Creator.
- Description.
- Interpretive notes.

Initially, these appear as separate pieces of information.

Introduce a simplified visual representation of the Surface Block.

The surface structure forms around the existing image.

The image itself remains unchanged.

The enriched object lifts slightly and turns over.

Its reverse side contains the associated metadata and research notes, arranged as editable document blocks.

Allow the viewer to read the reverse side.

Turn the object back to its original image surface.

The original image is still present.

Briefly reveal the compositional relationship: the image is a constituent of the richer structure.

**Narration**

> Consider an image.
>
> We might wish to record its source, its date, its subject, or our observations about it.
>
> Rather than treating those materials as a separate document, we can compose them with the image itself.
>
> By giving the image another surface, we create a richer intellectual object.
>
> The reverse side can contain the apparatus through which we investigate and understand the image.
>
> Yet the original image remains an image.
>
> It has become something more without ceasing to be what it was.

**Pedagogical objective**

Introduce extension through composition.

The image does not require a bespoke image-specific reverse-side mechanism.

Its new expressive possibilities arise through its participation in a more general compositional structure.

Distinguish this from file conversion or replacement.

---

### Scene 07 — The generality of the operation

**Visuals**

Hold the enriched image composition.

Briefly reveal the image block and the surrounding surface structure as distinguishable components.

Then introduce a paragraph.

Apply the same general surface operation.

The paragraph acquires a reverse side containing working notes or supporting evidence.

Introduce another recognisable block, such as a diagram, and illustrate the same principle where supported by the implementation.

The surface operation should have a consistent visual signature across the different objects.

Do not linger on each example.

The important revelation is that the same operation is applicable to different kinds of material.

**Narration**

> The important thing is not simply that an image can be turned over.
>
> It is that the operation need not belong exclusively to images.
>
> A paragraph can acquire another surface. A diagram can acquire another surface.
>
> We need not independently invent the same capability for every kind of object.
>
> Different objects can participate in the expressive possibilities of a common compositional system.

**Pedagogical objective**

Reveal the generality of the block architecture.

The preceding scene establishes the practical benefit.

This scene explains why that benefit is architecturally significant.

---

### Scene 08 — The possibilities of a sentence

**Visuals**

Introduce a beautifully typeset draft letter.

The author is considering how to express a delicate sentiment.

Show the sentence:

“I was disappointed not to receive a reply.”

The author considers another formulation:

“I had hoped to hear from you sooner.”

Then a third:

“I appreciate that you have had other demands on your time.”

Initially, the alternatives appear as successive revisions, with each replacing the previous expression.

Briefly communicate how a creative decision can ordinarily result in the disappearance of its alternatives from the visible composition.

Restore the three formulations.

The sentence's block becomes a tabbed composition.

Each alternative occupies a separate tab at the same position within the letter.

Switch between the formulations.

The surrounding letter remains intact.

The alternatives remain available.

Use simple, restrained tab controls translated into the established pedagogical visual language.

**Narration**

> Mutability can also operate at the scale of a single thought.
>
> When writing a letter, we may consider several ways of expressing the same sentiment.
>
> Ordinarily, choosing one formulation means displacing another from the visible document.
>
> But the alternatives can themselves become part of the composition.
>
> We can preserve them in the very place where the creative decision is being made.
>
> The document can contain not only what we have chosen to say, but also the possibilities we have considered.

**Pedagogical objective**

Communicate the preservation of alternatives through composition.

The tabs are not merely a space-saving device.

They provide a local, deliberately authored record of creative possibilities.

Do not imply that this is an automatically recorded version history unless the implementation supports that behaviour.

The alternatives are intentionally preserved by the author.

## ACT IV — MUTABILITY LIBERATES SPATIAL THINKING

### Scene 09 — The burden of sequence

**Visuals**

Introduce a short essay containing a clear central argument.

Gradually add valuable intellectual material:

- A qualification.
- Supporting evidence.
- An objection.
- A contextual observation.
- A further possibility.

Initially, all the material is inserted into the same vertical sequence.

The document grows.

The central argument becomes increasingly difficult to apprehend.

Use consistent visual identifiers to distinguish the different bodies of information.

Do not depict the additional material as worthless clutter.

Its value should remain apparent.

Present the question:

**Must everything that belongs to a document belong to its primary reading sequence?**

**Narration**

> As our thinking develops, the material surrounding an idea can become increasingly rich.
>
> We accumulate evidence, qualifications, objections and possibilities.
>
> Each may deserve a place in our intellectual work.
>
> But if everything must occupy the same continuous sequence, that richness can come at the expense of clarity.
>
> Must everything that belongs to a document also belong to its primary reading sequence?

**Pedagogical objective**

Establish the intellectual problem of compulsory linearity.

Do not suggest that linearity itself is undesirable.

The problem is the absence of alternatives when another arrangement would better serve the material.

---

### Scene 10 — Thinking beside

**Visuals**

Return to the essay and its central argument.

Supporting material moves from the primary sequence towards the left margin.

An objection moves towards the right.

Additional observations gather beside the central block.

The central argument becomes easier to follow.

The associated material remains locally available.

Show the spatial relationship between the central idea and the ideas gathered around it.

Allow the author to reposition one of the marginal blocks, demonstrating that the spatial arrangement itself can be an expressive decision.

The left and right margins may suggest an informal organisation of evidence and objections in this particular example, but do not imply that Mutable imposes such meanings.

**Narration**

> We can place an idea beside another idea rather than after it.
>
> Supporting evidence might gather on one side of an argument, and objections on the other.
>
> Their proximity communicates a relationship without requiring them to interrupt the primary discourse.
>
> Space becomes part of our expressive vocabulary.
>
> We are not merely arranging information for aesthetic effect.
>
> We are using its arrangement to help us think.

**Pedagogical objective**

Communicate spatial thinking through left and right marginalia.

Distinguish spatial relationships from formally asserted semantic relationships.

The spatial organisation can be informal, exploratory and personally meaningful.

---

### Scene 11 — Thinking behind, otherwise and within

**Visuals**

Continue working with the same essay.

Move detailed working notes onto a reverse surface associated with the central block.

Gather alternative formulations into an inline tabbed composition.

Organise a substantial body of related material into a bounded page.

Each transformation should be visually continuous.

The viewer should recognise the material from Scene 09 throughout.

Briefly explore each new spatial arrangement:

- Turn over the reverse surface.
- Switch between the alternative tabs.
- Enter or reveal the bounded page according to the actual Mutable implementation.

Return to the primary composition.

The document is now more approachable, although its informational richness has been preserved.

**Narration**

> We can also think behind an idea, preserving its supporting material on another surface.
>
> We can think in alternatives, allowing several possibilities to occupy the same conceptual position.
>
> And we can think within boundaries, composing related material into manageable intellectual wholes.
>
> Each arrangement expresses a different relationship between information and attention.
>
> The quantity of information need not determine the complexity of its immediate presentation.

**Pedagogical objective**

Bring together marginalia, surfaces, tabs and pages as manifestations of spatial mutability.

Avoid turning the scene into a catalogue of editor controls.

The focus is on the intellectual purpose of each arrangement.

---

### Scene 12 — Complexity can be composed

**Visuals**

Present the original overloaded essay beside its newly composed form.

Use the persistent visual identifiers to account for every body of information.

Briefly reveal where the original material now resides:

- In the primary sequence.
- Beside the central argument.
- On a reverse surface.
- Within alternative tabs.
- Within a bounded page.

Return to the clearer composition.

Introduce the statements:

**Complexity need not be eliminated.**

**It can be composed.**

Then:

**Mutability liberates spatial thinking.**

**Narration**

> We have not solved the problem by discarding information.
>
> We have changed the relationships between its constituent parts.
>
> The material remains available, but it need not all demand our attention simultaneously.
>
> Complexity need not be eliminated.
>
> It can be composed.
>
> Mutability liberates spatial thinking.

**Pedagogical objective**

Provide a moment of synthesis.

The spatial freedom demonstrated in this act should now be understood as a consequence of compositional mutability.

Allow the finished composition to remain visible long enough for the viewer to appreciate the transformation.

## ACT V — BEYOND THE BOUNDARIES OF THE DOCUMENT

### Scene 13 — A document within a document

**Visuals**

Introduce an independent research document.

It contains recognisable textual and visual blocks.

Beside it, introduce other related intellectual materials:

- Another document.
- An archival image.
- Research notes.
- A relevant source.

Gradually assemble these into a larger Dossier composition.

The independent research document becomes a page or constituent within the Dossier, according to the implemented architecture.

Its internal structure remains recognisable.

The camera moves into the embedded document and reveals its original contents.

Then move back out to the larger Dossier.

Where supported, illustrate the document becoming independently available again.

**Narration**

> The same principle can operate at a larger scale.
>
> A document may be an intellectual whole in its own right.
>
> But that whole can also become a constituent of another composition.
>
> A Dossier can bring documents, images, observations and other material together around a common subject.
>
> What was previously a complete document can acquire a new role within a larger intellectual arrangement.
>
> Composition can operate upon the results of composition.

**Pedagogical objective**

Communicate recursive composition.

The document is not necessarily the highest level of intellectual organisation.

An object can be a whole in one context and a constituent in another.

Do not depict the embedded document as a mere hyperlink or flattened screenshot if its actual behaviour is richer.

Equally, do not imply shared live identity or synchronized transclusion unless supported by the implementation.

**Visuals**

## ACT VI — THE OPEN-ENDED OBJECT

### Scene 16 — More than a video

**Visuals**

Introduce a video block containing a short illustrative lecture.

The lecture might concern a subject such as the architecture of memory, classical rhetoric or another topic suited to scholarly investigation.

Initially, the video is an independent, one-sided media object.

- A textual transcript.
- Screenshots.
- Research observations.
- Bibliographic references.
- References to related videos.

These materials initially appear as separate blocks.

The video is wrapped in a surface composition.

Turn the enriched object over.

On its reverse side, introduce the associated material as a document composition.

**Transcript**

**Screenshots**

**References**

Switch to the screenshots.

Return to the transcript.

**Narration**

> Now consider a video.
>
> We may wish to study what is said in it, examine particular images, record observations, investigate its references, or connect its ideas to our wider knowledge.
>
> These materials can become part of a composition associated with the video itself.
>
> A reverse surface can contain an annotated transcript.
>
> Tabs can preserve screenshots, references and related material within the same intellectual context.
>
> The video acquires textual, semantic and scholarly dimensions.
>
> Yet it remains a video.
>
> We have not merely changed its appearance.
>
> We have extended what it can become.

**Pedagogical objective**

This is the principal synthesis of the explainer.

The video composition should demonstrate the interplay of:

- A common block primitive.
- Extension through wrapping.
- Additional surfaces.
- Inline alternatives.
- Document composition.
- Standoff annotation.
- Semantic connections.
- Spatial organisation.
- Selective presentation.

The viewer should recognise these not as unrelated features, but as consequences of a common architectural philosophy.

## ACT VII — THE PRINCIPLE

# 6. Production and animation principles

## A. Preserve identity through transformation

The original object should remain visually recognisable throughout each transformation.

Use consistent content, colours, boundaries and other visual identifiers.

When a new composition is formed, show the original object becoming a constituent of it.

When a composition is decomposed, show its constituents becoming independently available.

Avoid unexplained morphing that suggests the original object has been replaced or destroyed.

## B. Make general operations visually consistent

The same compositional operation should have a recognisable visual signature when applied to different objects.

For example:

- Wrapping an image and wrapping a paragraph should share a visual vocabulary.
- Embedding a block and embedding a document should reveal their conceptual affinity.
- Composition and decomposition should appear as complementary operations.
- Surface reversal should communicate the same underlying principle across different media.

This consistency helps the viewer discover the generality of the architecture without requiring technical explanation.

## C. Make the transformations spatially intelligible

Where possible, animate transformations continuously.

Let the viewer see material move from the primary sequence into a margin.

Let a surface visibly form around an existing object.

Let alternative expressions gather into a tabbed composition.

Let a document become a constituent of a larger arrangement.

Do not rely exclusively on crossfades between before-and-after states.

The transformation itself is the pedagogical event.

## D. Distinguish extension from conversion

When an object acquires additional capabilities through composition, do not suggest that its original media type has necessarily changed.

An image wrapped in a Surface Block remains an image within an enriched composition.

A video associated with an annotated transcript remains a video.

The distinction between the original object and the richer composition is central to the argument.

## E. Distinguish identity from presentation

A change of visible surface, selected tab, spatial arrangement or document focus does not necessarily imply a change to the underlying intellectual object.

Use animation to reinforce the distinction between:

- What exists.
- How it is arranged.
- What is currently visible.
- What capabilities are available through its composition.

## F. Avoid the feature-catalogue effect

Do not introduce the examples as a succession of product features.

Every scene must answer an intellectual question.

The examples are manifestations of mutability, not the subject of the video in isolation.

Do not add conventional formatting features, columns, tables or largely invisible structural mechanisms merely for completeness.

Container structures may be revealed when necessary to explain composition, but they should not become independent feature demonstrations.

## G. Avoid excessive philosophical abstraction

The narration should remain accessible to viewers unfamiliar with software architecture, digital humanities or hypertext theory.

Introduce philosophical propositions through concrete examples.

Do not require the viewer to understand object-oriented programming, recursive data structures, ontologies or implementation-specific terminology before appreciating the demonstrated transformations.

The sophistication should reside in the ideas, not in unnecessarily technical language.

## H. Preserve the established pedagogical identity

Although this explainer introduces a new page-based visual register, it should remain recognisably part of the Mutable concept series.

Retain continuity in:

- Narration style.
- Typographic hierarchy.
- Conceptual pacing.
- Semantic annotation conventions.
- Animation discipline.
- Opening and closing identity.

The change in visual register should feel motivated by the subject matter.

# 7. Implementation verification

Before committing the final storyboard to animation, inspect the current Mutable codebase and verify the precise behaviour of the mechanisms used in the examples.

Pay particular attention to:

- Independent block presentation and extraction.
- Embedding blocks within documents.
- Preservation of block identity and annotations during compositional operations.
- Surface Block and Reverse Block behaviour.
- Wrapping media blocks in surface compositions.
- Inline tabs and the preservation of alternative content.
- Left and right marginalia.
- Page composition and navigation.
- Document participation within Dossiers.
- The relationship between application blocks and document blocks.
- Annotation capabilities within Dialectic.
- Preservation of immutable conversational contributions.
- Nested surface, tab and document compositions.
- Extraction of annotated material from enriched compositions.

Distinguish carefully between:

1. Capabilities currently implemented.
2. Capabilities supported by the architecture but not yet fully exposed through the interface.
3. Longer-term architectural possibilities.

The explainer may articulate the broader design philosophy, but it must not present an unimplemented interaction as an existing product capability.

Where a proposed demonstration is not fully supported, adapt it to a verified equivalent that preserves the intellectual principle.

Do not undertake substantial unrelated application development merely to make a storyboard scene possible without first obtaining approval.

# 8. Acceptance criteria

A successful explainer should leave the viewer understanding that:

1. Mutability encompasses considerably more than editing content.
2. Textual and semantic structures can persist through certain forms of change.
3. The block is a fundamental compositional primitive in Mutable.
4. A document is an arrangement of blocks rather than necessarily the permanent boundary of its constituents.
5. Intellectual objects can acquire new capabilities through composition.
6. Enrichment need not require replacement or conversion.
7. The same general compositional operations can apply to different kinds of objects.
8. Creative alternatives can be preserved at the site of a decision.
9. Spatial arrangement can become an instrument of thought.
10. Informational richness need not entail compulsory visibility or an increasingly complex primary reading sequence.
11. Documents can participate in larger intellectual compositions.
12. Applications and documents can share a common architectural foundation.
13. Conversations can acquire additional intellectual apparatus while preserving their original contributions.
14. Different forms of media can participate in rich scholarly compositions.
15. The results of composition can themselves become available for further composition.
16. Intellectual objects need not be permanently confined to their original forms, contexts or purposes.
17. Mutable's slogan, “Same Thing. Many Faces.”, expresses a principle of identity persisting through transformation.

The viewer should understand these ideas without needing to learn the current Mutable interface.

# 9. Final production review

Review the finished video for:

- Conceptual accuracy.
- Implementation accuracy.
- Narrative coherence.
- Legibility.
- Narration synchronisation.
- Spatial intelligibility.
- Continuity of object identity.
- Appropriate pacing.
- Consistency of compositional operations.
- Visual continuity with the existing explainer series.
- Successful introduction of the new page-based visual register.

In particular, evaluate whether the viewer can recognise the shared principle connecting the individual examples.

If the finished video feels like a tour of Mutable's features, revise the transitions and narration until the common philosophical foundation becomes unmistakable.

The final impression should not merely be that Mutable provides unusual ways of arranging information.

It should be that Mutable explores a different conception of what an intellectual object can be.

**Mutability is not merely the freedom to change things. It is the freedom to change what things can become.**
